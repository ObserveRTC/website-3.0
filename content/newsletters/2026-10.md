Thanks to everyone who has subscribed to the ObserveRTC newsletter! This month, we added more context for investigating audio and session behavior, a new codec for smaller telemetry streams, and native Client Monitor support. We also continued our WebRTC monitoring series, moving from the bigger picture into the details of connectivity and recovery.

**PROJECTS**

## Client Monitor 4.10.0

Client Monitor 4.10.0 adds loopback audio input detection, audio-device metadata, and SDP monitoring. Together, these help explain cases where transport statistics look healthy but the wrong input device or negotiated media settings deserve investigation.

Audio diagnostics now distinguish frequent short concealment events from longer interruptions. The new interruption detector uses Chromium-specific statistics and reports unavailable inputs elsewhere. Upgrading also requires updating the renamed ConcealedSamplesDetector configuration, events, and fields; the old InventedSpeechDetector names have no aliases.

[Read the release notes](https://observertc.org/updates/client-monitor-js-4.10.0/).

## Observer 1.2.0

Observer 1.2.0 adds client session totals, RTT and score histograms, and summaries that survive peer connection closure. This gives session analysis a view beyond the latest active connection. The old RTT measurement buckets are replaced by the RTT histogram API.

A new call-level audio echo detector correlates participants’ audio activity and levels, with capture-device context when available. Its finding is suspected echo to investigate, rather than proof of the cause. Richer audio evidence also depends on what the producing client supplies.

[Read the release notes](https://observertc.org/updates/observer-js-1.2.0/).

## Native Client Monitor 0.3.0

This month, we started adding native binding support for Client Monitor through a C++17 port, with the monitoring core, generated sample types, and five detectors. It gives native WebRTC applications a sample-based path into ObserveRTC. Coverage is still partial: the initial release’s optional libwebrtc and libmediasoupclient bindings had not yet been compiled against those dependencies, and the default score calculator had not been ported.

Version 0.3.0 corrects libwebrtc timestamp conversion: elapsed times had been interpreted as 1,000 times too long, distorting rates and time-based detector behavior. It also moves stats adapter management to ClientMonitor and fixes stale outbound-track associations that could raise false dry-track findings. Integrations upgrading from older C++ samples need to account for the corrected stats timestamp units and breaking adapter API changes.

[Read the release notes](https://observertc.org/updates/client-monitor-cpp-0.3.0/).

## Introducing the Deflate Codec

The new Deflate Codec combines structural deltas, a field-name dictionary, and platform deflate compression to reduce ClientSample transport size. Thanks to Håvar Aambø Fosstveit for the contribution.

Encoding and decoding are asynchronous, and each stream needs its own stateful encoder/decoder pair. Delivery must remain ordered and lossless, with coordinated resets after reconnects. The published compression measurements describe a recorded-call fixture; actual savings depend on the samples you send.

[Read the release notes](https://observertc.org/updates/deflate-codec-introduced/).

## Introducing the ObserveRTC STUNner demo

The ObserveRTC STUNner demo brings mediasoup on Kubernetes behind one public TURN endpoint, with Client Monitor diagnostics and Observer call analysis. Deployment configuration, Grafana metrics, and a load generator make it a practical reference for examining the deployment alongside its media behavior.

[Read the release notes](https://observertc.org/updates/observertc-stunner-demo-introduced/).

**ARTICLES & GUIDES**

### WebRTC Monitoring: Are We Connected?

A practical guide to investigating failed joins, slow connection setup, path changes, and recovery. It follows the evidence through signaling, ICE, STUN and TURN, and DTLS, showing how ObserveRTC detectors help narrow the investigation—and why connection recovery and media recovery need to be tracked separately.

[Read the article](https://observertc.org/blog/webrtc-connectivity-monitoring/).

### WebRTC Monitoring: The Bigger Picture

This article connects connectivity, transport quality, media pipeline disruption, and perceived quality with the application context needed to interpret them. A frozen-video example shows how to follow evidence across packet arrival, frame assembly, decoding, and playback—and why detecting a symptom does not establish its cause.

[Read the article](https://observertc.org/blog/holistic-webrtc-monitoring/).

## What’s next

We’ll continue stabilizing Client Monitor’s detectors and explore monitoring for Voice AI applications.

We’re also working toward a better Stats Dashboard, with a focus on showing quality problems that require server-side processing to detect. Alongside that, we’re exploring MCP capabilities and cloud-based analysis support.
